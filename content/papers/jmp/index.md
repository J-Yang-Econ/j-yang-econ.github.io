##### Abstract:

This paper examines how three monetary policy instruments—the federal funds rate, forward guidance, and large-scale asset purchases—differ in their distributional effects on U.S. household consumption. Using Consumer Expenditure Survey data for 1992–2019, orthogonal high-frequency shocks, and cell-level local projections, it finds no detectable difference at the national level between the consumption responses of the lowest and highest income quintiles under any instrument. This average masks two differences. In the Northeast, expansionary asset purchases raise non-housing consumption more for the lowest quintile than for the highest from the second year on, a differential statistically distinguishable from the other regions. Among lower-income households, contractionary conventional interest rate policy lowers consumption more for renters than for mortgagors, whereas contractionary asset purchase shocks do the reverse, consistent with a cash flow channel through refinancing. Forward guidance has no consistent distributional direction. The distributional consequences of monetary policy depend on the instrument, the region, and the housing contract through which policy reaches households.
