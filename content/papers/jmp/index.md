##### Abstract:

This paper examines how three monetary policy instruments—the federal funds rate, forward guidance, and large-scale asset purchases—differ in their distributional effects on U.S. household consumption. Using Consumer Expenditure Survey data for 1992–2019, orthogonal high-frequency shocks, and cell-level local projections, this paper finds that the increase in non-housing consumption following expansionary asset purchases is larger for the lowest income quintile than for the highest, with this differential significant in the Northeast and statistically distinguishable from other regions. This difference is absent in the first year, emerges in the second, and persists into the third. Across housing contracts, conventional interest rate policy produces a larger consumption decline among lower-income renters than among mortgagors with the same income, whereas asset purchases produce a larger decline among mortgagors, consistent with a cash flow channel through refinancing rather than a dominant employment channel. The Northeast's prominence is consistent with housing's greater weight in local household budgets. Direct tests show that the three instruments are not interchangeable, and forward guidance has no consistent distributional direction. The choice of instrument itself carries distributional consequences.
